Why flexible cables use many fine copper strands

Cut open a flexible cable and you find many thin copper wires instead of one thick one. That is deliberate, and it changes how the cable should be handled.

The metal core of a cable that carries the current is called the conductor. It can be one solid copper wire, or many thin wires twisted together. Flexible cables use the second kind, and the reason lies in what happens to copper when it bends.

What bending does to copper

When a wire bends, the metal on the outside of the curve stretches and the metal on the inside is squeezed. Bend a thick solid wire once and it simply stays bent. Bend it back and forth at the same spot and the copper there grows harder, tiny cracks form, and the wire finally snaps. It is the same thing that happens when you bend a paper clip until it breaks.

A thin strand stretches far less when it bends, because there is very little metal on the outside of its curve. Twist many thin strands together and each one bends gently. The strands can also slide a little against each other. The result is a conductor that stays soft and copes with being bent many more times.

The same copper, shared out

What decides how much current a conductor can carry is the total area of copper in it, measured in square millimetres. A 1.5 sq mm conductor holds about the same copper whether it is one solid wire or a bundle of fine strands. Stranding makes little difference to how well the wire carries current. It changes how the copper is arranged.

Cable standards sort conductors into classes by how flexible they are, from a single solid wire up to very fine strands for cables that move all the time. The finer the strands, the more of them fit in the same area, and the more flexible the cable becomes.

Good to knowThe size printed on a wire, such as 1.5 sq mm, is the copper area of the conductor. Two wires of the same size can look different because of their insulation, so compare the printed size, not how thick the wire looks.

Why copper suits fine strands

Copper carries electricity very well, and it is ductile: it can be drawn out into very fine wire without breaking. Drawing makes the metal hard, so the wire is then annealed, which means it is heated to make it soft again. Soft, fine strands are what let a flexible cable bend over and over.

Where flexibility matters

  • House wiring, where wire is pulled through conduit with bends and corners, and folded into switch boxes.
  • Appliance leads and extension cords, which are moved, coiled and plugged in every day.
  • Panels and machines, where wiring runs through tight spaces, or moves and vibrates while the machine works.
  • Submersible pump cables, which are unwound, lowered down a borewell and pulled out again for service.

Working with stranded wire

  • Strip the insulation cleanly without nicking the strands. A nicked strand is a weak spot.
  • Never cut off a few strands to make the end fit a terminal. That leaves less copper to carry the current, and the connection can overheat.
  • Twist the strands together neatly, or better, crimp on a lug or ferrule so every strand is held under the screw.
  • Check that no stray strand is left outside the terminal, where it could touch the one next to it.
